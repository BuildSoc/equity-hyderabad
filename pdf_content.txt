Abstract
There is a growing emphasis on active transport, prompting cities to be redesigned as more
pedestrian-friendly environments. This shift has fueled interest in proximity-based planning models,
such as the X-minute city, which are increasingly promoted for their potential public health and
environmental beneﬁts. However, equity considerations in the assessment of such models are often
neglected or rely on local datasets that limit broader applicability. In response, we introduce a
method for evaluating equity under the X-minute city paradigm using open data and established
accessibility metrics and grounded in the ethical frameworks of egalitarianism and sufﬁcientarianism.
Applying our method to Athens, Stockholm, and Amsterdam, we assess equitable access to essential
services and amenities, including supermarkets, playgrounds, and public transport stations. Our
ﬁndings demonstrate that the choice of ethical framework plays a critical role in shaping equity
assessments, and that disaggregating by destination type reveals distinct spatial patterns of accessibility, shedding light on both the strengths and limitations of different urban areas. The application of our method also surfaces unexpected insights: Athens—despite its car-dependent
image—emerges as the most aligned with the X-minute city model, while Stockholm’s decentralized
form underscores the need to adapt time thresholds to local urban structure and behavior. Overall,
the method’s reproducibility and use of open-data support cross-city comparisons and contribute
to the evolving methodological toolkit for evaluating proximity-based planning.
Keywords
accessibility, proximity, equity, pedestrians, 15-min city

Introduction
Urban and transport planning discourse has increasingly shifted away from car-centric planning
paradigms toward proximity-based models (Buttner et al., 2024; Handy 2023; Lu and Diab 2023).
This shift seeks to address pressing concerns related to public health, environmental sustainability,
and urban resilience. Consequently, there has been heightened interest in urban planning models that
advocate for compact, mixed-use development, designed to enable residents to access essential
services and amenities—including parks, public transport, and healthcare—within a short walking
or cycling distance (Allam et al., 2023; Silva et al., 2023a).
Among such models, the X-minute city—most notably popularized as the 15-min city—has
become a prominent example (Logan et al., 2022; Weng et al., 2019). Within the X-minute city
paradigm, the overarching aim is to ensure that all residents can reach key destinations within an
X-minute walk or bike ride, in some cases supplemented by public transit options (Moreno et al.,
2021). Given the widespread promotion of proximity-based planning and the X-minute model in
policy agendas, various data-oriented methods and tools have been developed to assess its implementation. Typically, such approaches focus on assessing how equally different types of services
and amenities are accessible in locations within an urban area (Giuffrida et al., 2024; Rojas Rueda
et al., 2024).
Nevertheless, an exclusive focus on equal distribution can be limiting, particularly when it fails
to address whether all residents have sufﬁcient access to the most essential amenities required for
daily life (Lucas et al., 2016). As a result, the assessment of proximity-based models in terms of their
implications on spatial equity is often partial, and new initiatives embracing proximity planning
might fall into the trap of exacerbating inequity.
Certain studies have addressed equity considerations and provided valuable and in-depth insights
into equity in accessibility (Van Wee 2022). These contributions have advanced our understanding
of spatial inequities within urban contexts. However, many of these approaches rely on localized
datasets, constraining their replicability elsewhere. As such, there remains a need for standardized
methods that can assess equity within the X-minute city paradigm across a broader range of urban
settings, including those with limited data availability.
This work introduces a method for evaluating equity in X-minute cities by leveraging
open data and established accessibility measures. Building on the framework proposed by
Lucas et al. (2016), our approach combines the ethical principles of egalitarianism and
sufﬁcientarianism with cumulative accessibility metrics to assess spatial equity within the
X-minute city paradigm.
To demonstrate the method, we apply it to three European cities representing the South, Central,
and North of the continent—Athens (GR), Amsterdam (NL), and Stockholm (SE)—and evaluate
equity in accessibility within 5- and 15-min walking distances to essential services and amenities,
including supermarkets, playgrounds, and public transport stations. The results illustrate how the
proposed method enables the evaluation of accessibility within the X-minute city framework,
revealing spatial inequities that differ across destination types and time thresholds. They also
emphasize the inﬂuence of the underlying ethical assumptions—egalitarianism versus
sufﬁcientarianism—in shaping how equity is assessed and interpreted.
Applying the method across different amenities and urban contexts reveals distinct patterns of
accessibility gaps and potential areas of unmet need. In doing so, the analysis surfaces city-speciﬁc
dynamics. For instance, Athens, typically seen as car-oriented, demonstrates unexpectedly strong
potential for proximity-based planning, while Stockholm’s decentralized urban form results in nonlinear changes in equitable access across time thresholds. More broadly, this work aims to
demonstrate how open and reproducible methods can inform locally relevant, yet broadly comparable, equity evaluations of proximity-based planning models.

Related work
The X-minute city
Since the mid-20th century, urban and transport planning has largely prioritized cars, promoting
motorized mobility and contributing to degraded public spaces, spatial fragmentation, poor connectivity, and suburban sprawl (Mattioli et al., 2020). In response, cities are gradually addressing the
negative consequences of this model by tackling both global (e.g., climate change, resilience) and
local challenges (e.g., trafﬁc congestion, inadequate open space, and low road safety) (Kinigadner
et al., 2020).
Amid this shift, the X-minute city, often popularized as the 15-min city and documented by
Carlos Moreno in 2016 (Moreno et al., 2021), has re-emerged as a promising urban model. Rooted
in early 20th-century planning principles (Pozoukidou and Chatziyiannaki 2021), it envisions
residents accessing essential daily needs and amenities such as education, public spaces, and leisure
within a short travel time (e.g., 5, 10, 15 minutes), typically by walking or cycling (Knap et al.,
2023). The model gained global visibility in 2020, when Paris Mayor Anne Hidalgo adopted the 15min city as a central pillar of her campaign and began integrating it into the city’s planning strategy
(Willsher 2020). Since then, numerous other cities have followed, embedding similar principles into
their urban development plans (Pozoukidou and Chatziyiannaki 2021; Weng et al., 2019).
The X-minute city marks a shift from mobility- to accessibility-centered planning, aiming to
redistribute urban functions and reduce motorized travel demand (Gil Sola and Vilhelmson, 2019).
It emphasizes “ease of reaching desired activities” via active travel, supporting sustainable, decentralized, and polycentric development (Khavarian-Garmsir et al., 2023; Silva et al., 2023b). Its
implementation relies on four pillars: (a) density, to support viable local services; (b) proximity, to
ensure spatial coherence; (c) diversity, to encourage land-use mix; and (d) digitalization, to enhance
service delivery and engagement (Papadopoulos et al., 2023). Walkability, safety, and transport
infrastructure further shape destination reachability (Guzman et al., 2024; Pozoukidou and
Chatziyiannaki 2021).
Moving beyond conceptual appeal requires robust, replicable methods to accurately measure and
quantify what deﬁnes an “X-minute” neighborhood. Such methods are essential to systematically
assess spatial equity, identify service gaps, and monitor urban planning progress, particularly in
diverse and data-scarce contexts. Key measurement variables include the spatial distribution of
amenities, network-based travel times (rather than simple Euclidean distances), population density,
people’s needs and preferences, and mode-speciﬁc accessibility (walking, cycling, public transport).
Several recent studies have advanced the measurement of the X-minute city concept. Moreno
et al. (2021) proposed a framework centered on six key urban activities—living, working, commerce, healthcare, education, and entertainment—to evaluate proximity and daily access. Walkability has also emerged as a key metric: Rhoads et al. (2023) assessed the 15-min city through
detailed sidewalk infrastructure analysis, revealing that even pedestrian-friendly cities like Barcelona may fall short when infrastructure quality is considered. Caselli et al. 2022 developed a
method to explore 15 minute neighborhoods via assessing walking distances and resident population. Applying that method to Parma helped identify the service areas for different population
groups. Cardoso et al. (2024) developed a walkability index tailored to the context of Amsterdam by

Equity in accessibility
Despite increasing interest in proximity-based urban models and the X-minute city, equity considerations remain seriously underexplored. Existing assessment methods often reduce equity
considerations to simplistic measures of equal spatial access, overlooking the complex and
multifaceted nature of equity that is well established conceptually in urban research. In practice,
there is often a strong focus on the equal spatial distribution of resources, without adequately
addressing whether the diverse accessibility needs of different population groups are
sufﬁciently met.
In their seminal work, Lucas et al. (2016) argue that the discourse on accessibility and transport
planning has paid limited attention to socioeconomic dimensions and demographic factors of
accessibility, thus shaping social exclusion conditions. Appraising distributive justice into accessibility metrics, this work introduces a quantitative approach combining both egalitarian and
sufﬁcientarian principles to examine access inequity.
Recent studies have engaged with these concerns. While these contributions have signiﬁcantly
enriched the scientiﬁc debate, they often employ context-speciﬁc and non-replicable methods. For
example, studies such as Kelobonye et al. (2020a) introduce reﬁned metrics that account for
competition effects in service access, improving upon traditional cumulative opportunity models.
Nonetheless, these approaches frequently rely on detailed local datasets (e.g., household travel
surveys or administrative records) that may limit their transferability to other urban contexts.
Likewise, Poorthuis and Zook (2023) remark how 15-min city frameworks, when applied only to
dense urban cores, risk marginalizing suburban and rural populations that face structurally different
accessibility issues. These critiques call for methods that are both scalable and equity-centered,
allowing for comparisons across diverse urban environments and population groups.
In this line, some recent studies show compelling progress. For instance, Vale and Lopes
(2023) use pedestrian isochrones and pseudo-Gini coefﬁcients to evaluate intra-urban inequalities in 15-min walkable access across several European cities. Their outcomes indicate
that while many cities promote compact urban forms, substantial spatial inequities in access
persist within them. Similarly, Radics et al. (2024) propose a reproducible method for
mapping access to essential amenities via active travel in Seville, pointing out where
neighborhoods succeed or fall short of X-minute principles. Yet, even with these critical
contributions, there is still a lack of reproducible methods that explicitly incorporate equity
into the measurement of X-minute accessibility.
As Mouratidis (2024) critically argues, simplistic applications of the X-minute city model can
overlook factors such as age, mobility impairment or the availability of high-quality destinations
and therefore neglect inclusive spatial planning. What is more, broader reviews like Rojas-Rueda
et al. (2024) emphasize that even if the X-minute city has potential public health and sustainability
beneﬁts, it may also create new forms of exclusion when equity is truly appreciated. Consequently,
they manifest the need for tailored equity metrics and stakeholder engagement as a component of an
X-minute planning strategy.

Methodology
The proposed methodology aims to support the analysis of accessibility in X-minute cities, integrating equity considerations and ensuring replicability across diverse urban contexts. Figure 1
presents an overview of the analytical pipeline, which comprises two core components: (1) estimating accessibility to different types of destinations from locations across a city within an
X-minute walking distance and (2) evaluating equity in accessibility combining an egalitarian and a
sufﬁcientarian perspective. The entire method leverages openly available data applicable to a wide
range of countries and cities. The components of the pipeline, along with the data sources used, are
detailed in the following sections. An implementation of the proposed method is available as an
open-access interactive web tool, CThood, which can be accessed and explored at https://miliasv.
github.io/CThood/?city=athens.

Accessibility estimation
In this study, we operate under two key assumptions. First, we assess accessibility using an
isochrone-based cumulative measure, which counts the number of reachable destinations from a
given origin based on walking distance. This approach has been widely adopted in prior studies,
and its simplicity and ease of interpretation have also contributed to its popularity among
practitioners (Kelobonye et al., 2020b; Milias et al., 2024; O’Sullivan et al., 2000; Willberg et al.,
2023). Speciﬁcally, we adopt 5 and 15-min walking thresholds to delineate the area individuals
can reach from each origin-location. We selected these two thresholds to represent different
interpretations of the X-minute city paradigm. The 15-min threshold was chosen because of its
growing prominence in research and urban policy, as many cities and studies have adopted it in
recent years (Weng et al., 2019; Moreno et al., 2021; Willsher 2020; Pozoukidou and
Chatziyiannaki 2021; Caselli et al. 2022). Recognizing that the selection of any speciﬁc
threshold is inherently arbitrary, we also include the 5-min threshold to explore its effect on our
analysis. Furthermore, a 5-min walking distance may more accurately reﬂect the needs of
particular population groups, such as older adults, who prefer or require shorter walking trips
(Prins et al., 2014). Second, we assume a uniform walking speed of 1.26 m/s (or 4.54 km/h) across
the entire population. This value represents the average walking speed across age groups, as
reported by Schimpl et al. (2011).

Equity scores
For estimating equity-related scores of accessibility we follow a territorial-based approach, considering the locations that cover the areas of analysis, and not a population-based approach.

population demand, such as when aiming at spatial coverage (Vale and Lopes 2023). To estimate
location-speciﬁc scores and identify inequities in access, we adopt an approach inspired by Lucas
et al. (2016), applying two equity-based metrics grounded in the ethical principles of egalitarianism
and sufﬁcientarianism.
The egalitarian perspective encourages policies and interventions that aim to equalize the level of
accessibility across the selected locations. In its simplest form, it reﬂects the norm in accessibilityrelated tools. It emphasizes relative differences in access between locations or population groups,
without necessarily considering whether the level of access is sufﬁcient to meet individuals’ basic
needs. It may also serve to highlight disparities affecting the least advantaged members of society
(Martens 2012).
In contrast, the sufﬁcientarian perspective focuses on ensuring that everyone has access to a
minimum level of opportunities necessary to meet their basic needs. The primary challenge with this
approach lies in deﬁning what that minimum threshold should be. It must take into account both the
speciﬁc needs of individuals (a person-based dimension) and the characteristics of their local
environment (a context-related dimension), making it difﬁcult to establish an adaptable accepted
standard (Lucas 2012).
To estimate an accessibility score grounded in an egalitarian perspective, we utilize the number
of destinations accessible from each location, reﬂecting the principle that all areas should enjoy
comparable levels of access. This is realized in two ways. First, we compute type-speciﬁc accessibility scores—for example, for playgrounds, sports facilities, and other types of destinations—
revealing how equally different types of activities and amenities are distributed across the city.
Second, we use the Gini index (Gini 1936) and the Lorenz Curve to measure and explore the
equality in access. The Gini index has been widely used as a statistical measure of inequality and
receives values from 0 (all locations share an equal number of accessible destinations) to 1
(maximum inequality). The Lorenz Curve is used to show the cumulative share of locations ordered
by increasing number of accessible destinations (Brown 1994).
To estimate accessibility from a sufﬁcientarian perspective, we adopt two complementary
approaches. In the ﬁrst approach, we deﬁne a score based on whether a location has access to
at least one place from each predeﬁned category of destination types. The resulting score is
normalized to range from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates no access to any destination type, and
1 indicates access to at least one destination of each type. This continuous scale reﬂects the
degree to which a location lacks sufﬁcient access to essential services and amenities. Rather
than applying a binary classiﬁcation of sufﬁcient vs insufﬁcient access, we propose a
severity-based indicator that captures gradations of insufﬁciency. The categories considered
include a wide range of destinations: playgrounds, public squares, supermarkets (or access to
all four food-related categories: bakery, greengrocer, butcher, and seafood shop), cultural
venues, nightlife-related activities, sports facilities, public transport stops (bus, metro, or
tram), pharmacies, cafes/restaurants, and libraries.
In the second approach, we apply the sufﬁcientarian principle within thematic domains. We
deﬁne four thematic groups: mobility, leisure, daily needs, and family/children, each associated with
a subset of relevant destination types. Speciﬁcally, mobility is deﬁned as access to at least one metro,
bus, or tram stop; leisure includes access to a nightlife venue, cultural place, library, public square,
or cafe/restaurant; daily needs refer to access to a pharmacy and a supermarket (or access to all four
food-related categories: bakery, greengrocer, butcher, and seafood shop); and family/children
includes access to a playground, kindergarten, and public square.
We acknowledge that selecting a predeﬁned set of destination types and thematic groups involves
a degree of arbitrariness. However, this choice is intended to demonstrate the applicability of the
proposed methodology and its sensitivity to the types of destinations included. Relevant studies
have also adopted a similar approach for evaluating the X-minute city through the lens of

Data
Case-study cities: To illustrate the applicability of our approach, we selected three European capital
cities—Athens (GR), Amsterdam (NL), and Stockholm (SE)—representing Southern, Western, and
Northern Europe, respectively (Ofﬁce UNS, 1982). While Amsterdam and Stockholm each span over
200 km2, the Municipality of Athens covers a much smaller area (∼39 km2), whereas the Athens
Metropolitan Area is signiﬁcantly larger (∼2,928 km2). To ensure comparability in spatial extent, we
delineated a study area for Athens by aggregating adjacent municipalities within the Athens Metropolitan Area, resulting in a contiguous urban area of approximately 187.5 km2. This selected extent
aligns closely with the areas of Amsterdam (∼199.1 km2, excluding large water bodies) and
Stockholm (∼216.8 km2, excluding large water bodies). These three cities were selected not only for
their geographic diversity but also because they offer a meaningful basis for comparison. They share
key characteristics: all are European capitals with dense, mixed-use urban cores. Simultaneously, they
represent distinct regional and morphological contexts—Athens, representing Southern Europe, is
highly urbanized and compact; Stockholm, in Northern Europe, is more spatially dispersed and
interwoven with green and blue infrastructure; while Amsterdam, in Western Europe, is uniquely
shaped by its extensive canal network and organized land use-transport landscape.
Origin locations: To ensure complete spatial coverage of the study area, we employ Uber’s
H3 hexagonal grid system (Uber Technologies Inc. 2018). By setting the resolution level to 10, we
generate hexagonal cells that span approximately 152 m at their widest point, providing consistent
and ﬁne-grained spatial units across the entire area of interest. Accessibility is estimated using the
centroids of the hexagonal cells as origin locations. Cells with zero access to any of the selected
destinations within a 15-minute walking distance are excluded from the analysis, as they are
assumed to represent entirely rural areas, often corresponding to forests or water bodies. Removing
these cells allows for a more meaningful comparison between the built-up areas of the cities,
focusing the analysis on locations where human presence or development is at least plausible.
Pedestrian street network: The street network data originate from OpenStreetMap (OSM)
(OpenStreetMap Contributors 2021), an open-source user-generated mapping platform widely used in
studies focused on pedestrian accessibility (Al Shammas et al., 2023; Cohen and Dalyot 2021; Logan
et al., 2019). The OSM street network has been documented to exceed 40% completeness across
countries worldwide (Barrington-Leigh and Millard-Ball 2017), enabling the replication of our approach in cities beyond those analyzed in this study. OSM pedestrian street network includes all types
of streets except those designated exclusively for motorized trafﬁc, such as motorways, which are not
intended for pedestrian use. We extracted the pedestrian street network, using OSMnx (Boeing 2017),
a Python-based library designed for retrieving and analyzing street network data from OSM. By
setting network type = “walk,” we collect footpaths and pedestrian-designated streets while automatically excluding segments tagged as unsuitable for walking, similar to previous works (Milias and
Psyllidis 2022). To collected street network data we ﬁrst expanded the boundaries of the areas of
analysis using a buffer of 1 km to avoid the boundary effects. Data were collected in April 2025.
Destinations: The destinations included in our work are collected from OSM and span a diverse
range of types including playgrounds, public squares, supermarkets, grocery stores (i.e., greengrocer,
bakery, butcher, seafood), cultural venues (i.e., museums, galleries, art centers), nightlife-related
activities (i.e., pubs, bars, nightclubs, music venues, cinemas, and theaters), sports facilities, bus stops,
metro and tram stations, pharmacies, cafes/restaurants, and libraries. These categories were selected to
capture a broad spectrum of everyday needs and activities. The selection of destination types can be
customized to accommodate different contexts. The POI data were collected in April 2025.

whether basic access needs are met in each city.

Examining accessibility per destination type reveals certain similarities across the three cities.
For example, playgrounds, bus/train stations, and sports facilities consistently exhibit the lowest
Gini indices, indicating comparatively more equal access than other destination types in all cities.
Similarly, supermarkets and cafes/restaurants show moderate levels of inequality. However, notable
differences also emerge. Within the 5-min walking distance, access to pharmacies in Athens is
considerably more equal, with a Gini index of 0.66, compared to signiﬁcantly higher values of
0.91 and 0.93 in Amsterdam and Stockholm, respectively. Conversely, cultural venues show
relatively high inequality in Athens and Stockholm, with Gini indices near 0.90 at the 5-min
threshold and around 0.80 at the 15-min threshold. Amsterdam, by contrast, exhibits approximately
0.10 lower Gini values for cultural venues, reﬂecting greater equality in access.
To compare the impact of destination type on our results, Figure 2 displays the estimated Lorenz
curves for each destination type across the three case-study cities. The horizontal dispersion of the
curves reﬂects variations in access equality among destination types: the greater the separation, the
more pronounced the disparities. In contrast, tighter clustering of the curves indicates more uniform
accessibility scores across different types of destinations. In Athens, particularly at the 5-min
threshold, the curves exhibit notable divergence, highlighting substantial inequalities in access
across different types of destinations. For Amsterdam and Stockholm, the curves are more closely
aligned, suggesting comparatively balanced access.
Examining the impact of the time threshold reveals that access inequality is generally more
pronounced within the 5-min walkshed than within the 15-min walkshed, particularly for certain
destination types. For instance, Gini indices for playgrounds range from 0.62 to 0.74 at the 5-min
threshold but improve to 0.37–0.52 at 15 minutes. Similar patterns are observed for public squares
(0.81–0.96 vs 0.55–0.80), metro stations (0.92–0.98 vs 0.68–0.87), and sports facilities (0.62–
0.74 vs 0.32–0.52). The magnitude of improvement varies not only by destination type but also
across cities. Stockholm, for example, often demonstrates substantial reductions in inequality
when moving from a 5- to a 15-min threshold, with Gini indices dropping by up to 20% in several
cases—particularly for sports, grocery stores, and public squares. In contrast, Amsterdam shows
more modest improvements, suggesting less sensitivity to the walking time threshold.

Sufﬁcientarian-based accessibility
Figure 3 displays the distribution of locations according to their sufﬁcientarian-based accessibility
scores for all three cities. As outlined in the Methodology section, this score reﬂects the extent to
which a location has access to at least one destination of each selected type. Additionally, the spatial
distribution of these scores is presented in Figure 4, while Table 2 summarizes the percentage of
locations with access to at least one destination from each type or thematic group, by city and
walking distance threshold.
As shown in Figure 3, in most cases, the majority of locations do not meet the selected access
criteria. Athens, however, has many locations with high accessibility scores when the walking time
threshold is set to 15 minutes. Notably, 32.4% of locations in Athens achieve the maximum

15-minute walk—substantially outperforming Amsterdam and Stockholm.

that of Athens. At the 5-min walking threshold, most locations in both cities fall into the lowest

Discussion and concluding remarks
Egalitarianism and sufﬁcientarianism
Overall, accessibility scores from the egalitarian approach align with and complement those from
the sufﬁcientarian approach. For instance, Athens exhibits both the highest equality in accessibility
and the highest percentage of locations meeting the selected sufﬁcientarian conditions. This
suggests that, compared to Amsterdam and Stockholm, a greater proportion of locations in Athens
enjoy comparable access to a diverse range of destinations while meeting a minimum threshold of
accessibility.
Nevertheless, comparing the two approaches within Athens reveals that while access inequality is
relatively low within a 5-min walk (Gini index: 0.46), only 0.63% of locations offer sufﬁcient access to
at least one destination of each type—indicating limited service coverage. This aligns with Vale and
Lopes (2023), who noted that despite compact urban forms, signiﬁcant spatial inequalities in access
remain. Similar patterns emerge in Amsterdam and Stockholm: within a 15-min walk, access equality
remains moderate (Gini index around 0.57), yet the proportion of locations with sufﬁcient access is
relatively low, at 17.74% and 10.24%, respectively. These ﬁndings suggest that relying on a single
approach—either egalitarian or sufﬁcientarian—could underestimate or overlook important deﬁciencies in the X-minute city model’s ability to support both equal and sufﬁcient access.
In all three cities, sufﬁcientarian scores proved highly sensitive to the inclusion of speciﬁc
destination types. For example, the share of locations with sufﬁcient access to mobility versus
leisure destinations differs by more than 48% across cases. In most cases, locations across all three
cities failed to meet the sufﬁcientarian criteria—especially for leisure and family or child-related

destination types. While our thematic categorization is to some extent arbitrary, the pronounced
differences highlight the importance of developing theme-based scores that better reﬂect citizens’
needs. This is essential, as different areas may demonstrate unique strengths and deﬁcits that only
become meaningful when interpreted in the context of local preferences and demand.

Time threshold
The impact of shifting the temporal threshold from 5 to 15 minutes varies signiﬁcantly across the
three cities, indicating differing levels of sensitivity. In some cases, such as Amsterdam and Athens,
this shift results in a noticeable extension of access scores from central areas to surrounding
neighborhoods, albeit with differing magnitudes. In contrast, Stockholm presents a different spatial
pattern, with the emergence of distinct satellite centers rather than a gradual outward expansion.
This observation is consistent with the ﬁndings of Cats et al. (2015), who highlighted how
Stockholm’s regional planning has prioritized the development of satellite towns along rapid public
transport corridors. This observation is also conﬁrmed in the recent study of Costa et al. (2025) that
clustered Stockholm as a ”Multimodal Metropolises,” while Amsterdam belongs to an elite group of
cycling cities. Therefore, the change in equitable access between the two time thresholds is neither
uniform nor linear. The absence of a generalized spatial pattern makes it difﬁcult the development of
a conceptual model or method for evaluating cities based on a limited set of characteristics. The
above ﬁndings underline the importance of choosing an informed time threshold that aligns with the
mobility behaviors and urban form of each city.

The cities
A notable ﬁnding of this study is that, despite often being characterized as a car-dependent city,
Athens demonstrates the strongest potential for realizing the 5- or 15-min city model. It performs
best under both egalitarian and sufﬁcientarian frameworks and across nearly all destination types,
with the exception of cultural venues.
The compact urban form, high density, and mixed land use of Athens contribute signiﬁcantly to
its strong performance, setting it apart from the other two cities. It is important to note that this study
assesses territorial-based accessibility without accounting for population demand. As a result,
accessibility is evaluated in terms of spatial coverage, which tends to penalize sparsely built-up
areas—such as those commonly found in Stockholm—with lower accessibility scores. Following
Athens, Amsterdam ranks second overall and Stockholm third, despite the latter demonstrating
comparatively better access to mobility-related services.
It is also important to note that Amsterdam’s street network strongly supports active mobility,
particularly walking and cycling. In contrast, Athens lacks high-quality pedestrian infrastructure,
especially in peripheral areas. This may help explain why the commercial city center of Athens
reports the lowest walkability score among the three cities, falling behind both Amsterdam and
Stockholm (Bartzokas-Tsiompras et al., 2023). The discrepancy between our proximity-based
accessibility scores and walkability outcomes suggests that even when the distribution and density
of diverse destinations align with the X-minute city model, inadequate walking infrastructure can
hinder actual on-foot accessibility.
Interestingly, walking in Athens still accounts for approximately 12–16% of all trips, while the
average walking trip distances range from 500 m to 2.8 km (Andrinopoulou and Tzouras 2025).
This indicates a behavioral adaptation to the local urban fabric. Pedestrians have developed ways to
navigate and cope with challenging conditions in Athens (Karakitsou et al., 2025). In comparison,
Amsterdam records walking trip shares of over 25% (De Haas and Hamersma 2019), while
Stockholm’s combined walking and cycling mode share reaches around 22.1% (Kenworthy 2020).

Limitations and future directions
The proposed method centers on three key components, each of which presents certain limitations
and opportunities for further reﬁnement: (1) the measurement of accessibility, (2) the selection of
destination types, and (3) the measurement of equity.
First, accessibility can be measured using various methods, each with speciﬁc strengths and
limitations—including gravity-based, person-based, and utility-based measures (Handy and
Niemeier 1997; Levinson and Wu 2020). Depending on the context, different measures may be
more appropriate, and the proposed method can be adapted accordingly. More importantly,
evaluating pedestrian access requires considering additional factors that shape the walking experience. These include subjective factors such as perceived safety, comfort, and attractiveness, as
well as objective factors like elevation and temperature (Alfonzo 2005). Incorporating such elements can lead not only to a more nuanced understanding of access inequities but also a fairer city
comparison. For instance, Athens’s hilly terrain contrasts sharply with the ﬂat landscape of
Amsterdam. While accounting for elevation may have little effect for Amsterdam, it can significantly affect walking speed and willingness to walk in Athens, particularly for older adults or those
with mobility limitations.
Second, the selection and grouping of destinations ought to consider the needs and preferences of
the population groups within the city under study. As Mouratidis (2024) emphasizes, the deﬁnition
of a sufﬁcient 15-min city may vary signiﬁcantly across social groups. Incorporating an initial step
in the proposed method to capture the residents’ needs and preferences regarding essential destinations could enable a more informed, potentially weighted, selection of destinations. This, in turn,
could better reﬂect the extent to which the city addresses its population’s diverse needs within the
X-minute city framework. We also acknowledge that this study excluded certain key destinations,
such as primary schools, kindergartens, and workplaces, which play a fundamental role in everyday
accessibility. Future extensions of this work could incorporate these destination types to provide a
more comprehensive understanding of the X-minute city.
Third, this study employed the Gini index and Lorenz curve to evaluate equality in accessibility,
combining an egalitarian and sufﬁcientarian lens to assess spatial equity. While these measures are
widely used, they have also faced criticism for certain limitations in capturing the full complexity of
equity in urban contexts (Karner et al., 2024). The proposed framework could be expanded to
incorporate alternative equity metrics aligned with different ethical theories, including utilitarian,
sufﬁcientarian, and egalitarian approaches. In addition, our method focused on a territorial-based
analysis and did not account for population-based inequities. However, it can be further extended by
integrating population datasets—either from local sources or global gridded data—to better capture
how accessibility varies across different population groups.
This work offers a reproducible, open-data-based method for evaluating the potential of cities to
support the X-minute model. By allowing comparisons across diverse urban contexts while remaining adaptable to locally speciﬁc data and needs, the proposed method contributes to a growing
methodological toolkit for proximity-based planning. Beyond providing a comparative framework,
our approach aims to spark a broader conversation about the role of open data in shaping equitable,
proximity-based planning.
